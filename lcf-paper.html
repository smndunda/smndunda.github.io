Executive Summary

While foreign currency is important and required for debt repayments, import payments, and protecting domestic currencies against chaotic macro events, frontier African economies face persistent currency mismatches when borrowing in foreign currency[cite: 23]. This over-reliance on foreign currency, especially during times of volatility, brings about significant currency mismatch risk[cite: 24]. These risks frequently lead to debt defaults, constrained macroeconomic policy, and severe economic crises[cite: 25].

Local currency financing (LCF) is floated as a strong mediator of these problems[cite: 26]. It ensures debt repayments are made in the same currency as the lending amounts, enhances economic resilience to global shocks, and contributes to the deepening of local financial markets[cite: 26].

Despite these benefits, most lenders face significant hurdles to accessing LCF, including high floatation costs, internal biases from lenders, and insufficient domestic capital[cite: 27]. This paper focuses on assuaging these fears by outlining financial instruments, country-level debt management practices, and policy reform[cite: 28]. Furthermore, consideration is given to technological enablers fundamental to bolstering trust in local domestic markets and promoting financial robustness over the next decades[cite: 29].

PART I: Introduction

Africa’s Historical Reliance on Foreign Debt

In recent years, African governments have increased their reliance on foreign debt to fund critical projects[cite: 32]. This has been exacerbated by global financial shocks, including the 2008 crisis, the 2020 pandemic, and geopolitical conflicts that have slowed growth potential in the African frontier region[cite: 32].

The IMF notes that since 2010, foreign currency-denominated bonds have been the fastest-growing source of financing for frontier low-income countries, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa, with Eurobond issuances nearly tripling since 2012[cite: 33].

As of 2023, external debt reached USD 1.2 trillion, representing approximately 60% of the region's total public debt stock[cite: 34]. Between 2008 and 2023, external debt increased from 18.8% of GDP to 41.6% of GDP[cite: 35]. However, based on IMF forecasts, debt as a percentage of GDP is set to decline from 66.8% in 2024 to around 63.5% in 2028 due to strong macroeconomic predictions for that period[cite: 36].
